# Perinatal Mental Health UX Research & Design Findings

**Date:** August 31, 2026  
**Focus:** Rainuka Oberoi's therapy practice specializing in perinatal mental health  
**Scope:** UX best practices, trauma-informed design, color psychology for calming anxious clients

---

## Executive Summary

Perinatal mental health digital interventions require trauma-informed, equity-centered design that addresses the unique emotional needs of pregnant, postpartum, and grieving individuals. Key research shows that users have diverse personas with distinct design needs, requiring personalization, accessibility, cultural sensitivity, and reassuring tone.

---

## Section 1: Perinatal Mental Health UX Best Practices

### 1.1 User Personas & Diverse Needs

Research from NIH/PMC identifies **four distinct user personas** with unique design implications:

1. **"Resilient" Users**
   - Background: Stigma, discrimination, limited resources
   - Design Need: Destigmatizing language, resource lists (doulas, midwives), anonymity options
   - Content Approach: Build trust before medical terminology

2. **"Lonely" Users**
   - Background: Unplanned pregnancies, no partner, isolation
   - Design Need: Virtual community, anonymity, avoid couple-focused messaging
   - Content Approach: Normalize diverse family structures

3. **"Overwhelmed" Users**
   - Background: Busy schedule (childcare, work), managing multiple health concerns
   - Design Need: Brief, scannable content with "learn more" options
   - Content Approach: Don't feel burdensome; offer downloadable content for no-data scenarios

4. **"Aware" Users**
   - Background: Mental health history, connected to care providers
   - Design Need: Professional terminology, partner involvement options
   - Content Approach: Share data with healthcare team; integrate with clinical workflow

### 1.2 Critical Design Considerations

**Content Personalization**
- Allow users to specify key life circumstances (planned vs. unplanned pregnancy, partner status, gender identity)
- Reflect these choices throughout the interface to reduce isolation/exclusion
- Avoid assumptions that can trigger grief (e.g., "Is this your first child?")

**Information Privacy & Data Sharing**
- Perinatal users have concerns about CPS involvement and data security
- Offer anonymous username/login options
- Provide transparent choices for information sharing
- Include downloadable content for users with unreliable internet

**Language & Destigmatization**
- Replace clinical terms with plain language (e.g., "gel-like blobs of thickened blood" not "coagulated")
- Validate emotional experiences; acknowledge discomfort even if "normal"
- Avoid platitudes ("You should be grateful," "At least you can try again")
- Honor grief and anxiety as valid, coexisting with joy/hope

**Support Beyond Digital**
- Integrate information about doulas, midwives, peer support
- Include resources for trauma, grief, and reproductive loss
- Partner involvement options for users who want them
- Connections to culturally aligned care

### 1.3 Pregnancy Loss & Perinatal Grief Specific UX

From research on perinatal loss interventions (e-Perinatal Project):
- **Trigger sensitivity:** Content about "healthy babies" is deeply painful for those who've experienced loss
- **Identity integration:** Loss becomes part of parental identity; allow users to process this
- **Grief + anxiety coexistence:** Users experience joy AND profound loss simultaneously
- **Early intervention:** Digital support in first weeks/months after loss is critical

**Recommendations:**
- Offer content that acknowledges both loss and possibility of future pregnancy
- Include trauma-informed grounding techniques
- Normalize that grief is not linear; offer multiple pathways
- Use inclusive language around "parents" not "mothers" alone

## Section 2: Trauma-Informed Design for Perinatal Mental Health

### 2.1 Core Principles

**1. Safety & Predictability**
- Clear, consistent navigation
- No surprise triggering content
- Explicit content warnings for sensitive topics
- User control over what content appears

**2. Respect for Autonomy**
- User choice in how they engage
- Options to skip content without judgment
- Flexible pacing; no required frequency of interaction
- Ability to download/revisit content offline

**3. Nervous System Awareness**
- Perinatal experiences are held in the body
- Design should respect the nervous system
- Slow pacing; no rushed or urgent language
- Allow processing time between sections

**4. Validation Over Reassurance**
- Acknowledge the reality of anxiety, grief, rage
- Normalize postpartum rage (not just depression/anxiety)
- Include voices of people with lived experience
- Avoid toxic positivity

### 2.2 Accessibility & Inclusion

**Visual Design**
- High contrast for readability
- Generous whitespace to reduce cognitive load
- Avoid overstimulating colors
- Offer light/dark mode for sensitive eyes

**Content Accessibility**
- Plain language; short paragraphs
- Headings for scannability
- Captions for video
- Transcripts for audio

**Representation**
- Diverse body types, races, family structures
- Include LGBTQ+ perspectives
- Represent diverse cultural values around pregnancy/motherhood

## Section 3: Color Psychology for Calming Anxious Clients

### 3.1 Evidence-Based Color Effects

**Blue** (Primary calming color)
- Associated with: Calmness, trust, stability, serenity
- Physiological effect: Lowers heart rate and blood pressure
- Caution: Avoid very dark blues (can feel depressing); lighter/softer blues are ideal
- Best use: Primary background, focus areas, meditation/grounding content
- **Scientific basis:** Blue has shown measurable effects on reducing state anxiety

**Green** (Secondary calming color)
- Associated with: Nature, growth, healing, harmony
- Physiological effect: Promotes sense of balance and renewal
- Caution: Avoid very bright/neon greens (can be energizing)
- Best use: Accent color, call-to-action buttons, success states, recovery themes
- **Scientific basis:** Nature-inspired greens (sage, muted tones) are restorative

**Warm Neutrals** (Grounding/safe)
- Cream, warm gray, soft taupe
- Associated with: Safety, comfort, home
- Best use: Background, text, secondary content
- Benefit: Creates sense of being held/contained

**Warm Accent** (For hope/compassion)
- Soft peachy-warm orange (#d97757) or rose tones
- Associated with: Warmth, compassion, hope
- Best use: Sparingly; for emotionally important content
- Caution: Don't overuse; can become overstimulating

### 3.2 Colors to Avoid for Anxious Populations

- **Red:** Activates fight-or-flight response; use only for critical alerts (not buttons)
- **High-saturation yellows:** Can trigger anxiety or agitation
- **Harsh contrast:** Black on white can feel stark; use warm neutrals instead
- **Overly bright/neon tones:** Overstimulate nervous system

### 3.3 Current Palette Alignment

**Rainuka's current color system:**
- Sage (#82a86c, #6b934f): Calming, growth-oriented ✓
- Cream-50 (#faf9f7): Warm, safe background ✓
- Warm-gray (#7a6f68, #5e534a): Grounding, readable ✓
- Accent-warm (#d97757): Compassionate warmth ✓

**Verdict:** The existing palette is thoughtfully chosen for perinatal care. Enhancement opportunity: **Ensure the most anxious content (pregnancy loss, anxiety sections) uses even softer, more muted versions of sage/green to maximize calming effect.**

### 3.4 Application for Perinatal Copy & Messaging

Even with calming colors, the *tone of copy* matters enormously:

- **Validation over reassurance:** "What you're feeling is real" (not "Don't worry, it will pass")
- **Permission language:** "You're allowed to feel grief and joy" (not "Look on the bright side")
- **Specificity:** Name the experience ("Pregnancy loss grief", "Postpartum rage") rather than generalizing
- **No toxic positivity:** Avoid "Everything happens for a reason" or "At least you can try again"

## Section 4: Design Enhancement for Rainuka's Perinatal Page

### 4.1 Recommended Enhancement: Reassuring Copy & Visual Hierarchy for Emotional Support

**Rationale:** The current perinatal page has excellent content and structure. The next level of refinement is making the *most vulnerable moment* (booking call, deciding to reach out) feel maximally safe and reassuring through:

1. **Softer, warmer CTA language** that validates before asking action
2. **Visual emphasis on "You're not alone"** through thoughtful hierarchy
3. **Trauma-informed microcopy** throughout (small reassuring phrases)
4. **Subtle color refinement** in emotional support sections

### 4.2 Specific Changes Implemented

**Change 1: Hero Section CTA Language**
- FROM: "Book a Free Consultation" (transactional)
- TO: "Start Your Free Consultation" (invitational, safe language)
- Rationale: "Start" feels less like signing up for an obligation; more like beginning a journey

**Change 2: "What You'll Get" Section - New Reassurance Component**
- Add a subtle visual callout box with background color `var(--burgundy-100)` (very soft, barely there)
- Include micro-reassurance: "This space is judgment-free. Your timeline, your pace."
- Emphasizes safety without being preachy

**Change 3: CTA Button in Hero**
- Refine button text to: "Schedule Your Free 30-Min Call"
- Specificity (30-min) reduces anxiety about time commitment
- More concrete, less abstract

**Change 4: Emotional Support Emphasis**
- Highlight the "Who I Help" section with a subtle background color shift to `var(--burgundy-50)`
- This section lists perinatal-specific experiences (loss, anxiety, etc.)
- Visual prominence signals: "Your specific experience is recognized here"

**Change 5: Hero Subheading Validation**
- Enhance the tagline: "Pregnancy, loss, postpartum. What you're carrying isn't weakness."
- This is already good; refine with subtle visual emphasis (slightly larger, warmer text color)

## Section 5: Research-Informed Recommendations for Future Iterations

1. **User Testing:** Conduct interviews with perinatal users (pregnant, postpartum, loss survivors) on copy tone
2. **Accessibility Testing:** Test with users who have anxiety/postpartum anxiety disorder
3. **Trauma-Informed Review:** Have Rainuka and LMSW colleagues review for unintended triggers
4. **Community Representation:** Include diverse voices in testimonials/case studies
5. **Loss Sensitivity:** Add subtle content warnings for sections touching on pregnancy loss/grief
6. **Partner Content:** Create parallel resources for non-birthing partners (often overlooked)

## Color Psychology & Perinatal Care Summary

| Color | Psychology | Best Use | Caution |
|-------|-----------|----------|---------|
| **Soft Sage** (#82a86c, #9dbb8d) | Calming, nature, growth | Background, sections on hope/recovery | Avoid very dark sage (feels institutional) |
| **Very Soft Sage** (#b5c7aa, cream) | Extreme safety, comfort | Content on loss, trauma, anxiety | Can feel empty if overused |
| **Warm Gray** (#7a6f68, #c9b8ac) | Grounding, professional, readable | Body text, neutral sections | Avoid looking clinical/cold |
| **Warm Accent** (#d97757) | Compassion, warmth, hope | Sparingly for emotional CTA buttons | Don't overuse; reduces calming effect |
| **Cream** (#faf9f7) | Safety, "home", comfort | Primary backgrounds | Needs contrast; pair with warm gray text |

**Key Insight:** Perinatal users respond better to *layers of reassurance* (color + microcopy + specificity) than to any single design element. The combination of calming color, validating language, and trauma-informed structure is more powerful than perfecting one variable.
