## DRAFT 1: Maya - Perinatal Anxiety → Grounded Motherhood

### The Situation

Maya came to therapy at 8 weeks postpartum, sent by her OB/GYN after she confessed she hadn't slept a full night since her daughter was born—and it wasn't the baby keeping her awake. Every time the baby slept, Maya's brain spun. Racing thoughts. Catastrophes. Intrusive images of something happening to her daughter. Then the thought that froze her: *What if I actually wanted something bad to happen? What if that means I'm not fit to be her mom?*

She'd been a senior software engineer before maternity leave. Problem-solving was her identity. But she couldn't problem-solve her way out of her own mind.

### Before Therapy: The Spiral

"I was terrified," Maya said in our first session. "Not of being a parent in the normal way. I mean, I'm sleep-deprived and my body feels weird and breastfeeding is harder than anyone told me. But that's not the terror. The terror is that I'm having these thoughts and that means something is broken in me."

She'd googled her symptoms obsessively—postpartum depression, postpartum psychosis, intrusive thoughts, harm OCD. Each article made her more convinced something was fundamentally wrong with her. She started catastrophizing about losing custody, her partner leaving because he'd realize she wasn't capable, her daughter growing up damaged because of her broken mother.

The insomnia wasn't just anxiety. It was hypervigilance. She'd set her phone to wake her every 20 minutes to check on the baby. When she wasn't checking, she was convinced the baby would die in the night.

Her partner said gently: "The baby is fine when we both check on her together. You've seen that." But Maya couldn't trust her own perception anymore.

### Rainuka's Approach

Rainuka didn't start with relaxation techniques or CBT worksheets. She started here:

**"What you're experiencing—these intrusive thoughts—they're one of the most common presentations of postpartum anxiety. They feel like proof that you're dangerous or unfit. They're not. They're anxiety. And we can work with anxiety."**

That single distinction shifted something. Maya had been fusing her thoughts ("What if I wanted to hurt her") with her values ("I'm a bad mother"). Rainuka separated them.

Then came the nervous system work. Not meditation—Maya couldn't sit still long enough. But grounding techniques that actually worked because they didn't require her brain to be calm first. 5-4-3-2-1 sensory awareness when the spirals started. Box breathing before bed. A written protocol her partner could help her follow: "When you're in the spiral, call me. Say these three things. We'll do the breathing."

But the deepest work was this: Rainuka validated that the exhaustion was real, the fear was understandable, and Maya's drive to be a good mother was actually part of what was keeping her trapped. "You care so deeply," Rainuka reflected. "That's not the problem. The problem is your brain is in threat mode because of hormones + sleep deprivation + the weight of responsibility you feel. We're not fixing you. We're helping your nervous system regulate."

She also named what Maya needed to hear: "Your intrusive thoughts are not desires. They're not predictions. They're anxiety creating false alarms. Your brain is trying to protect your daughter by imagining every possible threat. That's actually the part of you that cares about her. It's just in overdrive."

### After Therapy: The Shift

Three months in, the night terrors stopped. Not because Maya forced them away, but because her nervous system slowly realized: the baby is fine. The home is safe. Checking every 20 minutes isn't necessary.

"I slept a full six hours last week without waking up to check," Maya reported. "I woke naturally because the baby cried. And my first thought wasn't catastrophe—it was just, okay, she needs something. I can help."

The intrusive thoughts didn't disappear entirely—she still gets them sometimes, maybe a few times a week. But now she can recognize them: "That's the anxiety alarm, not the truth." She doesn't fuse with them. Doesn't assume they mean she's broken.

By month six, she'd returned to part-time work. The first week back, she had a moment of panic—leaving her daughter at childcare. Rainuka had prepared her: "That panic makes sense. You're separating from someone you love. That's not a sign you shouldn't go back to work. That's a sign you love her."

Today, 10 months postpartum, Maya says: "I'm not the person I was before I had her, but I'm not the terrified person I was at 8 weeks either. I'm someone who can hold both: I'm her mother and I'm still myself. The intrusive thoughts happen sometimes and I don't believe them. I can sleep. I can be present with her instead of terrified around her."

Her partner added: "She's back. Not the pre-baby version, but I have my wife back. And she's not drowning in her own head anymore."

### Why This Story Resonates

- **Speaks to high-achieving women** who expect to manage everything, including their own neurobiology
- **Addresses the shame** that comes with postpartum intrusive thoughts (most mothers never talk about them)
- **Shows Rainuka's expertise** in perinatal-specific anxiety (not generic anxiety therapy)
- **Outcome isn't perfection:** It's functional capacity + self-compassion + distinguishing thoughts from truth
- **Practical signals:** Partner involvement, specific techniques, realistic timeline

## DRAFT 2: Jordan - ADHD Diagnosis at 30 → Career Realignment

### The Situation

Jordan was 30 when he got diagnosed with ADHD. By then, he'd already:
- Dropped out of college twice
- Failed at three corporate jobs (each time, he "just couldn't focus")
- Been told repeatedly that he "wasn't living up to his potential"
- Built a narrative that he was lazy, undisciplined, lacking willpower

The diagnosis came because his partner finally said: "I think you might have ADHD. I've been reading about it and... it's you."

Jordan's first response was relief. *Finally, an explanation.*

His second response was grief so sharp it surprised him.

### Before Therapy: The Self-Blame

"My entire adult life, I thought there was something wrong with me," Jordan said in our first session. "Not just wrong. *Fundamentally* wrong. I'd get a job—good jobs, okay pay—and within six months I'd be behind. Emails piling up. Deadlines missed. My boss would say, 'Just stay organized,' and I'd think, *I'm trying.* But I wasn't, in a way I could explain or fix."

He'd tried everything. Planners. Apps. Getting to the office early. Staying late. The problem was that traditional office work requires sustained attention on things that don't interest him, in an environment designed for people whose brains work differently than his.

But he didn't know that then. He thought he was broken.

The grief when he got diagnosed was compound:
- Grief for the jobs he'd "failed"
- Grief for the college degrees he didn't finish
- Grief for the identity he'd built as "someone who can't stick with anything"
- But also: relief that it wasn't a character flaw. It was neurology.

He came to therapy wanting to figure out how to function in "normal" jobs. What he needed was permission to build a different life.

### Rainuka's Approach

"I hear that you're grieving," Rainuka said early on. "And I want to honor that. You've spent 15 years blaming yourself for something that was never about blame. That's real loss. But here's what I want to ask: What if your ADHD isn't a problem you need to fix, but a reality you need to build around?"

They didn't start with coping strategies for corporate jobs. They started with understanding: How does Jordan's brain work? What kinds of tasks hold his attention? What environments drain him? What would a career look like if it was *designed* for ADHD neurology, not *in spite of* it?

Rainuka also named something Jordan hadn't let himself think: "You're not lazy. Lazy is a choice. You have ADHD. Your executive function works differently. That's not laziness. That's neurotype."

The work became: What if he stopped trying to work like a neurotypical person and started building work around how he actually functions?

Jordan had always been good with people. Quick thinking. Crisis problem-solving (because urgent tasks activate his reward system). But sustained administrative work made him feel like he was moving through mud.

Rainuka asked: "What if you freelanced? Took on project-based work where urgency gives you natural dopamine? Built your own schedule? Outsourced the admin stuff?"

It sounded reckless. Jordan had debt. Responsibilities. He couldn't just *change everything*.

But the alternative was staying in a job that made him feel broken every single day.

### After Therapy: The Realignment

Jordan took the leap. Started freelancing 8 months into therapy. First year was rough—the financial instability triggered anxiety. But he had strategies now. He understood his own needs. He built systems that worked with his brain, not against it:

- Deadlines and urgent work keep him engaged (he found clients who needed quick turnarounds)
- He outsourced accounting to a CPA and admin to a VA
- He built in accountability: weekly check-ins with a business coach (not because he needed policing, but because external structure helped)
- He gave himself permission to work in bursts (not the 9-to-5 myth that never worked for him)

A year in, Jordan said: "I sleep better than I ever have. Not medicated sleep—just, I'm not spending all night spiraling about how I'm failing. I'm doing work that actually interests me, I'm making decent money, and when I have a deadline, my ADHD is an asset, not a liability."

The grief didn't disappear. Sometimes he thinks about the college degree he didn't finish, the years he spent hating himself. But now he contextualizes it: "I was operating with incomplete information. Now I know why I work the way I do. And I've built a life that fits, instead of trying to change myself to fit the life."

His partner noticed: "He's like a different person. Not because he's medicated or 'fixed.' Because he's not fighting himself anymore. He's actually *himself* now."

### Why This Story Resonates

- **Speaks to late-diagnosed adults** (increasingly common, especially for ADHD-I where hyperactivity isn't visible)
- **Addresses the grief moment** (many miss this—diagnosis isn't just relief, it's also loss)
- **Shows expertise in ADHD + career fit** (not just "manage your ADHD better in a normal job")
- **Outcome is concrete:** Changed career model, better sleep, reduced self-blame, actual joy in work
- **Narrative shift:** From "I'm broken" to "My brain works differently and I've built accordingly"

## DRAFT 3: Priya - ADHD Rejection Sensitivity → Relationship Clarity

### The Situation

Priya and her partner had been together six years. They fought about everything. The frustrating part: the fights weren't really about the thing they were fighting about.

Partner was annoyed about dishes in the sink: Priya would spiral into "He's disgusted with me. This relationship is falling apart. He's going to leave."

Partner had a work deadline and seemed distant: Priya convinced herself "He's losing interest. I'm too much. He regrets us."

Partner corrected Priya gently about something she'd said: Priya heard total rejection and would shut down for days.

In therapy intake, Priya said: "I don't know if our relationship is actually broken or if I'm just crazy and reading everything as rejection."

### Before Therapy: The Feedback Loop

Priya had been diagnosed with ADHD in college. She was medicated, did well academically, and thought she had it handled. But what she didn't know—what most people with ADHD don't know—is that many ADHDers also have "Rejection Sensitive Dysphoria" (RSD).

RSD isn't low self-esteem. It's a neurological hypersensitivity to perceived rejection, criticism, or disappointment. For Priya, it meant:

- Criticism felt like total rejection
- Ambiguity felt like abandonment
- Disappointment felt personal ("He's mad at me" not "He's frustrated about a situation")
- She'd catastrophize instantly and completely

Her partner loved her and was patient, but he was exhausted. Every minor conflict became a multi-hour emotional crisis where Priya needed constant reassurance. By year five, he'd started withdrawing emotionally—which, of course, Priya interpreted as proof he was leaving.

They were in a painful feedback loop: Priya's RSD triggered perceived rejection → she'd spiral → partner would reassure her → temporary relief → next perceived rejection → spiral again.

Neither understood why. Priya thought she was "too sensitive" or "emotionally needy." Her partner thought maybe they just weren't compatible.

### Rainuka's Approach

First, Rainuka named what was happening: "I want to introduce you to something called Rejection Sensitivity Dysphoria. It's common in ADHD and it's not a character flaw—it's neurology. Your brain perceives rejection more intensely than neurotypical brains do. When your partner is frustrated, your nervous system reads it as total rejection. That's not weakness. That's your wiring."

Just naming it shifted something. Priya wasn't crazy. She wasn't too sensitive. Her brain was doing its job—overprotecting her from perceived abandonment.

Then came the skills:

1. **Validation first, interpretation second:** When her partner seemed distant, Priya learned to ask before spiraling: "Hey, you seem frustrated. Is this about me or is this about your work stress?" Instead of assuming and catastrophizing.

2. **Differentiation:** Partner is annoyed about X ≠ Partner is done with Priya. These are separate facts. Rainuka taught Priya to literally separate them: "He's frustrated about dishes. He's not frustrated about me. His frustration is about the situation, not my worth."

3. **Pause the spiral:** When Priya felt rejection creeping in, she'd use a grounding statement: "This is RSD. My brain is trying to protect me from abandonment by imagining the worst. This isn't data. This is fear." Then she'd ask her partner, rather than assume.

4. **Partner education:** Rainuka also worked with Priya on how to explain her RSD to her partner so he understood: "When I ask for reassurance, it's not because you did something wrong. It's because my brain perceived threat and I need help recognizing that threat isn't real."

### After Therapy: The Repair

Three months in, the fighting style shifted. Not because the conflicts disappeared, but because Priya stopped *escalating* them.

Partner: "I'm frustrated about the dishes."
Priya (old response): *Spirals internally, interprets as total rejection, becomes defensive or withdrawn*
Priya (new response): "Okay, that makes sense. I'll get them tonight. Is anything else going on?"

The difference was enormous. The conflict was about dishes. It stayed about dishes. And once it was resolved, it was actually resolved—not a three-day emotional aftermath.

Six months in: "We're arguing less, but we're also more honest," Priya noted. "Because I'm not in survival mode, I can actually think about what he's saying instead of just hearing rejection."

Her partner: "I can see her working to stay present. She's not assuming the worst. We're actually fighting about real things now, not about her fear of abandonment. And honestly? Knowing what RSD is helps me too. I don't take it personally when she spirals. I just remind her: this is your brain, not the truth."

A year in, they reported genuine pleasure in each other's company again. Intimacy returned. Priya said: "I'm not 'fixed.' I still have RSD. But I'm not controlled by it. And our relationship feels like a choice we're both making, not like I'm desperate and holding on."

### Why This Story Resonates

- **Niche expertise signal:** RSD is increasingly discussed but poorly understood; showing Rainuka knows this positions her as cutting-edge in ADHD care
- **Couples impact:** Shows how individual ADHD neurology affects relationships
- **Outcome is practical:** Fewer fights, more real conversations, actual intimacy
- **Speaks to people who think they're "too emotional"** or their relationships are "doomed" because of sensitivity
- **Normalizes neurodiversity** in relationships (not "fix yourself to be a better partner" but "understand yourself + build communication around your actual neurology")
